**Short answer:** decide what the battery must do first — use more of your own solar, cut demand peaks, earn from peak/off-peak price spreads, or keep the lights on. Then size it from your load data for that job. Sizing to the solar array alone usually gives the wrong answer.

## 1. Pick the job

| Main job | What sets the size |
| --- | --- |
| Solar self-consumption | Typical daily solar surplus you would otherwise export |
| Peak shaving | Height and duration of the demand peaks you want to remove |
| Price arbitrage | Hours of the peak price window × the load in that window |
| Backup | Critical load × hours of autonomy needed |

Most commercial systems combine two jobs, often self-consumption plus peak shaving.

## 2. Look at the data

Twelve months of electricity bills show the tariff structure and seasonal swings. Interval data (15- or 30-minute readings) show when peaks happen and how much midday solar would be surplus.

## 3. A worked example

A factory runs a 400 kWp rooftop system. On weekends, when production stops, about 900 kWh of solar would be exported each day. On weekdays the surplus is small, but there is a two-hour evening peak at a high tariff.

- A **215 kWh** cabinet such as the [C&I Storage Cabinet 215 kWh](/products/ci-storage-cabinet-215/) can absorb part of the weekend surplus and cover the weekday evening peak.
- Doubling to two cabinets captures more weekend surplus, but adds little on weekdays. Whether it pays depends on the export price.

## 4. Check the economics

Compare the yearly saving per extra kWh of storage with its cost. The first kWh are the most valuable. Each additional block is used less often, so returns fall as the battery grows.
